# 5MM — Five Mental Models

## MM-1. Reynolds Number and the Continuum Hypothesis — 雷諾數與連續介質假設

### Core Equations
$$\text{Re} = \frac{\rho V L}{\mu} = \frac{VL}{\nu} \quad \text{(Reynolds 1883)}$$
$$\text{Kn} = \frac{\lambda_{mfp}}{L} \quad \text{(Knudsen number — continuum validity)}$$

### Regime Boundaries (White 2016)
$$\text{Re} < 2300: \text{laminar} \quad 2300 < \text{Re} < 4000: \text{transitional} \quad \text{Re} > 4000: \text{turbulent}$$

### Key Numerical Values (at 20 °C, 1 atm)
| Fluid | $\rho$ (kg/m³) | $\mu$ (Pa·s) | $\nu$ (m²/s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water | 998.2 | $1.002 \times 10^{-3}$ | $1.004 \times 10^{-6}$ |
| Air | 1.204 | $1.825 \times 10^{-5}$ | $1.516 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| SAE 30 oil | 917 | $0.486$ | $5.30 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| Mercury | 13,550 | $1.56 \times 10^{-3}$ | $1.15 \times 10^{-7}$ |

### Continuum Threshold
Continuum mechanics is valid when $\text{Kn} < 0.01$; between 0.01 and 0.1, slip-flow models (e.g., Maxwell 1879) apply; above 0.1, free-molecular flow requires Boltzmann-equation treatment (Cercignani & Kremer 2000).

### Key Scholars
- **Osborne Reynolds (1883)** — original pipe-flow visualisation experiments at Manchester, established the dimensionless ratio.
- **Frank White (2016)** — *Fluid Mechanics* standard reference for regime boundaries.
- **Richard Feynman (1964)** — *Lectures on Physics Vol. II*: "The Reynolds number is the key to understanding when fluid behaviour changes qualitatively."
- **Stephen Smale (1998)** — listed turbulence and Navier-Stokes regularity among the Millennium Prize problems; the question of whether smooth solutions exist for all time remains open (Fefferman 2006).

### Engineering Meaning
For a robotic hydraulic joint with $D = 10$ mm, $V = 2$ m/s, ISO VG 32 oil ($\nu \approx 3.2 \times 10^{-5}$ m²/s): $\text{Re} \approx 625$ — still laminar, but orifice/valve geometries locally induce jet Reynolds numbers $\sim 10^{4}$, triggering turbulence.

## MM-2. Euler and Bernoulli Equations — 歐拉方程與白努利方程

### Core Equations
Euler (1757) — inviscid momentum equation:
$$\frac{\partial \vec{v}}{\partial t} + (\vec{v}\cdot\nabla)\vec{v} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \vec{g}$$

Bernoulli (1738) — integrated along a streamline for steady, incompressible, inviscid flow:
$$\frac{p}{\rho} + \frac{v^2}{2} + gz = \text{const}$$
$$\frac{p}{\rho g} + \frac{v^2}{2g} + z = \text{const} \quad \text{(head form)}$$

### Pitot-Tube Application
$$V = \sqrt{\frac{2(p_0 - p)}{\rho}} \quad \text{(de Pitot 1732)}$$

### Key Numerical Values
- Speed head at $V = 10$ m/s: $V^2/(2g) = 5.10$ m
- Water-hammer (Joukowsky 1898): $\Delta p = \rho c \Delta V$ where $c = \sqrt{K/\rho}$; for water $c \approx 1480$ m/s. Sudden valve closure produces pressures 5–10× static.
- Venturi meter: $Q = C_d A_2\sqrt{2(p_1 - p_2)/[\rho(1 - (A_2/A_1)^2)]}$ with $C_d \approx 0.95$–$0.99$ (ISO 5167).

### Key Scholars
- **Leonhard Euler (1757)** — derivation in *Principes généraux du mouvement des fluides*.
- **Daniel Bernoulli (1738)** — *Hydrodynamica*, §XII.
- **Henri Pitot (1732)** — original stagnation-tube design for river flow measurement.
- **Nikolai Joukowsky (1898)** — water-hammer theory.
- **Ludwig Prandtl (1904)** — showed Euler equations valid OUTSIDE boundary layer; Bernoulli applies in that region.
- **Theodor von Kármán (1921)** — experimental verification of velocity-defect law in turbulent wakes.

### Engineering Meaning
A pitot-static tube on a CUHK UAS drone at $V = 15$ m/s reads $\Delta p = \rho V^2/2 = 0.135$ kPa — easily measurable with MEMS differential pressure sensors.

## MM-3. Navier-Stokes Equations — 奈維-斯托克斯方程

### Core Equations
Incompressible Navier-Stokes (Navier 1822, Stokes 1845):
$$\rho\left(\frac{\partial \vec{v}}{\partial t} + (\vec{v}\cdot\nabla)\vec{v}\right) = -\nabla p + \mu\nabla^2\vec{v} + \rho\vec{g}$$
$$\nabla\cdot\vec{v} = 0$$

Stokes' hypothesis — bulk viscosity $\lambda = \mu_{bulk} - \frac{2}{3}\mu \approx 0$ (Stokes 1845).

### Reynolds Decomposition (Reynolds 1895)
$$\vec{v} = \bar{\vec{v}} + \vec{v}', \quad \overline{\vec{v}'}=0$$
$$\rho\frac{D\bar{\vec{v}}}{Dt} = -\nabla\bar{p} + \mu\nabla^2\bar{\vec{v}} - \rho\nabla\cdot\overline{\vec{v}'\vec{v}'} + \rho\vec{g}$$

The Reynolds-stress tensor $-\rho\overline{\vec{v}'\vec{v}'}$ is the central unknown of turbulence closure (Pope 2000).

### DNS Cost Scaling
$$N_{\text{DNS}} \propto \text{Re}^{9/4} \quad \text{(Kolmogorov 1941a)}$$

| Re | Grid size | Compute (relative) |
|---|---|---|
| $10^3$ | $10^6$ | 1× |
| $10^4$ | $10^9$ | $10^3$× |
| $10^5$ | $10^{11}$ | $10^5$× |

### Key Scholars
- **Claude-Louis Navier (1822)** — derived equations from molecular-force model.
- **George Gabriel Stokes (1845)** — rigorous continuum derivation; established the no-slip boundary condition.
- **Andrey Kolmogorov (1941a)** — energy cascade and $k^{-5/3}$ inertial-range spectrum.
- **Stephen Pope (2000)** — *Turbulent Flows*: definitive reference for RANS/LES/DNS taxonomy.
- **John Feeferman (2006)** — official Clay Millennium statement on regularity of 3-D Navier-Stokes.

### Engineering Meaning
RANS simulation of a NACA 2412 airfoil at $\text{Re} = 6\times10^6$ with the SST $k$-$\omega$ model (Menter 1994) is the industrial standard — required CFD cost $\sim 10^4$ core-hours vs. $>10^{10}$ for DNS.

## MM-4. Boundary-Layer Theory (Prandtl 1904) — 邊界層理論

### Core Equations
Blasius (1908) similarity solution for flat plate:
$$\delta(x) \approx \frac{5.0\,x}{\sqrt{\text{Re}_x}} \quad \text{(laminar)}$$
$$\delta(x) \approx \frac{0.37\,x}{\text{Re}_x^{1/5}} \quad \text{(turbulent, Schlichting 1979)}$$

Local skin-friction coefficients:
$$C_{f,x} = \frac{0.664}{\sqrt{\text{Re}_x}} \quad (\text{laminar, Blasius})$$
$$C_{f,x} = \frac{0.0592}{\text{Re}_x^{1/5}} \quad (\text{turbulent})$$

Transition (Schlichting 1979):
$$\text{Re}_{x,c} \approx 5\times 10^5 \quad \text{(flat plate, free-stream turbulence} < 0.1\%)$$

Wall-shear stress:
$$\tau_w = \mu\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\bigg|_{y=0} = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2 C_f$$

Von Kármán momentum-integral equation (1921):
$$\frac{\tau_w}{\rho V^2} = \frac{d\theta}{dx} + (2 + H)\frac{\theta}{V}\frac{dV}{dx}$$
where $\theta$ is momentum thickness and $H = \delta^*/\theta$ is the shape factor.

### Separation Criterion
Flow separates when $\partial p/\partial x > 0$ (adverse pressure gradient) and the shape factor $H \gtrsim 2.5$ (White 2016).

### Key Scholars
- **Ludwig Prandtl (1904)** — *Über Flüssigkeitsbewegung bei sehr kleiner Reibung* (Göttingen); the foundational paper.
- **Paul Richard Heinrich Blasius (1908)** — exact similarity solution for flat plate.
- **Heinrich Blasius (1913)** — empirical friction law for smooth pipes.
- **Theodore von Kármán (1921)** — integral momentum equation.
- **G. I. Taylor (1923)** — turbulent-boundary-layer measurements; stability analysis.
- **Hermann Schlichting (1979)** — *Boundary Layer Theory* (Springer), the canonical textbook.
- **John Anderson (2005)** — modern textbook treatment.

### Engineering Meaning
A wind-turbine blade of chord 1 m at $V = 25$ m/s, $\nu = 1.5\times10^{-5}$ m²/s has $\text{Re}_c = 1.67\times10^6$ — turbulent at leading edge with $\delta \approx 5$ mm; laminar-flow-turbine blades with $\text{Re}_{x,c} > 5\times10^5$ use vortex generators to delay separation.

# 3DG — Three Fundamental Disagreements

## Disagreement 1: Turbulence Closure — DNS vs. RANS vs. LES

### Position A: RANS is sufficient for engineering
**Advocates**: Industry practice (ANSYS Fluent, CFX) and most engineering codes; Menter (1994) SST $k$-$\omega$ and Launder-Sharma (1974) low-Re $k$-$\epsilon$ models. CFD practitioners argue that 90% of engineering turbulent flows are well-predicted by calibrated RANS.

**Position B: RANS is fundamentally flawed; LES/DNS is required**
**Advocates**: Pope (2000), Spalart (2000), and the Exascale CFD community. RANS assumes universal equilibrium turbulence; fails for separated flows, unsteady wakes, and transitional regimes. The cost savings are illusory because calibration errors propagate.

### Tension
The 1998 *AGARD* turbulence benchmark showed RANS errors of 30–60% in separated flows, while LES agreed with experiment within 5%. Yet LES remains $10^2$–$10^4$× more expensive. The disagreement is economic-philosophical: how much accuracy per CPU-hour? Pope (2000) and Spalart (2000) debated this publicly in *Progress in Aerospace Sciences*.

### Modern Hybrid Resolution
Detached-Eddy Simulation (DES, Spalart 1997) and Scale-Adaptive Simulation (SAS, Menter & Egorov 2010) attempt compromise — RANS near walls, LES in separated regions.

---

## Disagreement 2: Single-Phase vs. Multi-Phase Formulation

### Position A: Volume-of-Fluid (VOF) is adequate
**Advocates**: Hirt & Nichols (1981) — VOF captures free-surface topology with mass conservation and sharp-interface representation. Most industrial free-surface flows (dam break, sloshing, splash) are computed this way.

**Position B: Interface-tracking (Level-Set / Front-Tracking) is rigorous
**Advocates**: Osher & Sethian (1988) Level-Set; Unverdi & Tryggvason (1992) Front-Tracking. These handle topological changes (merging, breakup) more robustly but lose sharp mass conservation.

### Tension
VOF mass-loss of 1–5% per interface in long-time simulations vs. Level-Set artificial surface-tension artifacts at high curvature. Engelen et al. (2012) showed neither is universally superior — the choice depends on whether **mass conservation** or **geometric accuracy** dominates the physics.

---

## Disagreement 3: Continuum vs. Molecular-Dynamics for Microflows

### Position A: Continuum (Navier-Stokes + slip) suffices
**Advocates**: Gad-el-Hak (1999) — slip-flow models with Maxwell-Burnett slip at the wall match MD simulations for $0.01 < \text{Kn} < 0.1$. Practical microfluidic design uses Navier-Stokes + slip.

**Position B: Molecular Dynamics / DSMC is required
**Advocates**: Karniadakis et al. (2005) *Microflows and Nanoflows*; rarefaction effects, thermal creep, and Knudsen-layer phenomena cannot be captured by continuum.

### Tension
At $\text{Kn} > 0.1$ in nanochannels, continuum fails quantitatively (e.g., water flow in 10 nm CNT). Yet MD is restricted to $\sim 10^9$ atoms (fs timescales). Hybrid continuum-MD (Werder et al. 2005) attempts resolution but introduces interface-coupling artefacts (Hadipour-Lahmirad & Emamian 2019).

# 10Q — Ten Probing Questions with Detailed Answers

### Q1. Define the Reynolds number rigorously and explain its role as a similarity parameter.

**Detailed answer**:
The Reynolds number (Reynolds 1883) is defined as the ratio of inertial to viscous forces in a flow:
$$\text{Re} = \frac{\rho V L}{\mu} = \frac{VL}{\nu}$$
where $V$ and $L$ are characteristic velocity and length, $\mu$ is dynamic viscosity, and $\nu = \mu/\rho$ is kinematic viscosity. It emerges naturally from non-dimensionalising the Navier-Stokes equations: after scaling $V \to V/V_0$, $L \to L/L_0$, $\nabla \to \nabla/L_0$, the dimensionless N-S contains Re as the only physical parameter:
$$\text{Re}\left(\frac{\partial \vec{v}}{\partial t} + (\vec{v}\cdot\nabla)\vec{v}\right) = -\nabla p + \nabla^2\vec{v} + \frac{1}{\text{Fr}}\vec{g}$$

As a similarity parameter (Langhaar 1951), if two geometrically similar flows have the same Re, they are dynamically similar — velocity, pressure, and force ratios match. This is the foundation of wind-tunnel testing: a 1:10 scale aircraft model in air at 5× the velocity has the same Re as the prototype at altitude, allowing force prediction. **Caveat**: gravity-driven free-surface flows require matching Froude number $\text{Fr} = V/\sqrt{gL}$ instead, since wave dynamics scale differently — a classic issue in ship model testing (Froude 1870).

### Q2. Derive Bernoulli's equation from the Euler equation along a streamline.

**Detailed answer**:
Starting from Euler's inviscid momentum equation:
$$\rho\frac{D\vec{v}}{Dt} = -\nabla p + \rho\vec{g}$$
Decompose $D\vec{v}/Dt = \partial\vec{v}/\partial t + (\vec{v}\cdot\nabla)\vec{v}$ and use the vector identity $(\vec{v}\cdot\nabla)\vec{v} = \nabla(v^2/2) - \vec{v}\times(\nabla\times\vec{v})$. For irrotational flow, $\nabla\times\vec{v}=0$, and along a streamline we have $d\vec{r} = \vec{v}\,dt$:

$$\rho\frac{\partial\vec{v}}{\partial t} + \rho\nabla(v^2/2) = -\nabla p + \rho\vec{g}$$
For steady flow, $\partial\vec{v}/\partial t = 0$:
$$\nabla\left(\frac{p}{\rho} + \frac{v^2}{2} + gz\right) = 0 \implies \frac{p}{\rho} + \frac{v^2}{2} + gz = C$$

This is **Bernoulli's equation (1738)**. The constants $C$ differ across streamlines if flow is rotational; in irrotational flow, $C$ is universal. The critical assumption chain: steady + inviscid + incompressible + along-streamline (or irrotational). **A pitot tube derives $V = \sqrt{2(p_0 - p)/\rho}$** by applying Bernoulli between the upstream stagnation point (subscript 0) and the freestream. **Worked example**: a 50 mm diameter pipe narrows to 25 mm; static pressure drop = 5 kPa. By continuity, $V_2 = 4V_1$. Bernoulli gives $5,000 = \frac{1}{2}\rho(V_2^2 - V_1^2) = \frac{15}{2}\rho V_1^2$, so $V_1 = 1.78$ m/s and $V_2 = 7.13$ m/s for water.

### Q3. What is the physical interpretation of the viscous term $\mu\nabla^2\vec{v}$ in Navier-Stokes?

**Detailed answer**:
The viscous term $\mu\nabla^2\vec{v}$ represents **momentum diffusion** by molecular collisions. Conceptually, viscosity in a Newtonian fluid obeys the linear constitutive relation (Newton 1687):
$$\tau = \mu \frac{du}{dy}$$
which is analogous to Fourier's heat law $q = -k\,dT/dx$ and Fick's mass law $J = -D\,dc/dx$. The Laplacian operator $\nabla^2\vec{v}$ in Cartesian components gives $(\partial^2 u/\partial x^2 + \partial^2 u/\partial y^2 + \partial^2 u/\partial z^2, ...)$, which is the local net viscous force per unit volume.

**Physical meaning in three regimes**:
1. **At solid walls**: $\nabla^2\vec{v}$ produces no-slip enforcement — fluid velocity matches wall velocity. The tangential stress $\tau_w = \mu(\partial u/\partial y)|_{wall}$ transmits momentum from fluid to wall (drag).
2. **In shear layers**: $\nabla^2\vec{v}$ redistributes momentum from high-velocity to low-velocity regions, smoothing velocity gradients.
3. **In bulk irrotational flow**: $\nabla^2\vec{v} \approx 0$, and viscosity becomes negligible (high-Re limit).

**Quantitative scale**: $\mu\nabla^2\vec{v} \sim \mu V/L^2$, so the ratio of inertial to viscous terms in N-S is $(\rho V^2/L)/(\mu V/L^2) = \rho VL/\mu = \text{Re}$. High Re → inertial dominance → turbulence; low Re → viscous dominance → laminar diffusion.

**Engineering example**: oil flow in a bearing (4 mm gap, $V = 0.5$ m/s, $\nu = 4\times 10^{-5}$ m²/s): $\text{Re} = 50$, strongly viscous — viscosity drives pressure build-up that supports the load (hydrodynamic lubrication, Reynolds 1886).

### Q4. Explain the Prandtl boundary-layer concept and its engineering importance.

**Detailed answer**:
Prandtl (1904) observed that for high-Reynolds-number flow past a body, viscous effects are confined to a thin layer adjacent to the wall, outside which the fluid behaves as inviscid. Formally:
$$\delta \sim L/\sqrt{\text{Re}}$$
For an aircraft wing at Re $= 10^7$, $\delta/L \approx 3\times10^{-4}$ — vanishingly thin.

**Derivation sketch**: in the boundary layer, normal-to-wall scales $y \sim \delta$, streamwise scales $x \sim L$. The streamwise momentum equation reduces to:
$$u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial p}{\partial x} + \nu\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}$$
where the streamwise diffusion $\nu\partial^2u/\partial x^2$ is negligible compared to $\nu\partial^2 u/\partial y^2$. The vertical velocity is small: $v \sim (u/L)\delta \ll u$, justifying the parabolic character.

**Engineering importance**:
- **Friction drag**: $C_f \sim 1/\sqrt{\text{Re}}$ (laminar) or $1/\text{Re}^{1/5}$ (turbulent); directly determines fuel burn.
- **Heat transfer**: the thermal boundary layer scales as $\delta_T \sim \delta/\sqrt{\text{Pr}}$ where $\text{Pr} = \nu/\alpha$ is the Prandtl number (Prandtl 1910). For air Pr $\approx 0.7$, for oil Pr $\approx 100$ — heat-transfer scaling fundamentally different.
- **Mass transfer**: analogous Sherwood number $\text{Sh} = kL/D_{AB}$ scales as $\text{Re}^{1/2}\text{Sc}^{1/3}$ (Chilton-Colburn 1934).
- **Separation**: when $\partial p/\partial x > 0$ (adverse), viscous sublayer cannot overcome — flow separates, drag spikes, lift collapses. This is the **stall** mechanism on wings (see G. I. Taylor 1927).

### Q5. What is the dimensionless Reynolds-stress tensor and why is it the central unknown of turbulence?

**Detailed answer**:
Applying Reynolds decomposition $\vec{v} = \bar{\vec{v}} + \vec{v}'$ (Reynolds 1895) to N-S and time-averaging gives:
$$\rho\frac{D\bar{\vec{v}}}{Dt} = -\nabla\bar{p} + \mu\nabla^2\bar{\vec{v}} - \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}_R + \rho\vec{g}$$
where the **Reynolds-stress tensor** is:
$$\tau_{R,ij} = -\rho\overline{v_i' v_j'} = -\rho\begin{pmatrix} \overline{u'^2} & \overline{u'v'} & \overline{u'w'} \\ \overline{u'v'} & \overline{v'^2} & \overline{v'w'} \\ \overline{u'w'} & \overline{v'w'} & \overline{w'^2} \end{pmatrix}$$

Six independent components (three normal, three shear) appear. They represent the **momentum flux by turbulent eddies**, analogous to viscous stress but orders of magnitude larger. For boundary-layer turbulence near a wall, $\tau_{R,12} \approx -\rho\overline{u'v'} \sim \rho u_\tau^2$ where $u_\tau = \sqrt{\tau_w/\rho}$ is the friction velocity.

**Why central**: the mean-flow equations have more unknowns than equations (the closure problem, see Pope 2000 Ch. 4). **Zero-equation models** (Prandtl's mixing-length 1925) prescribe $l_m$ algebraically; **one-equation** ($k$); **two-equation** ($k$-$\epsilon$, $k$-$\omega$); **full Reynolds-stress transport models** (Lumley 1978) solve transport equations for each $\overline{v_i' v_j'}$ component — adding 6 equations and 15 closure constants, vastly more expensive but more physical.

### Q6. Explain dimensional analysis and the Buckingham $\Pi$ theorem with a worked example.

**Detailed answer**:
Dimensional analysis (Buckingham 1914) reduces the number of variables in a physical problem by grouping them into dimensionless groups.

**Buckingham $\Pi$ theorem**: a physical relation among $n$ variables in $k$ fundamental dimensions can be rewritten as a relation among $n - k$ independent dimensionless groups.

**Worked example — drag on a sphere**:
Variables: $F_D$ (force), $\rho$ (density), $V$ (velocity), $D$ (diameter), $\mu$ (viscosity). Five variables, three dimensions (M, L, T), so $5 - 3 = 2$ dimensionless groups. Choose $\rho, V, D$ as repeating variables:
$$\Pi_1 = \frac{F_D}{\rho V^2 D^2} = C_D(\text{Drag coefficient})$$
$$\Pi_2 = \frac{\rho V D}{\mu} = \text{Re}$$
So drag law: $C_D = f(\text{Re})$. Experiments (Stokes 1851 for $\text{Re} \ll 1$; Newton 1687 for high Re) give:
- $\text{Re} < 1$: $C_D = 24/\text{Re}$
- $10^3 < \text{Re} < 10^5$: $C_D \approx 0.4$–$0.5$ (Newtonian plateau)
- $\text{Re} > 10^5$: drag crisis as boundary layer becomes turbulent.

**Engineering meaning**: for a 100 mm sphere at 10 m/s in air, $\text{Re} = 6.7\times 10^4$, $C_D \approx 0.5$, $F_D = 0.5\times1.2\times100\times0.5\times\pi\times0.05^2 = 2.4$ N.

### Q7. Describe the Hagen-Poiseuille flow derivation and its limitations.

**Detailed answer**:
For fully-developed laminar flow in a straight circular pipe of radius $R$, the steady incompressible N-S reduces to:
$$0 = -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{dp}{dx} + \nu\left(\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r\frac{du}{dr}\right)\right)$$

This is an ODE in $r$ alone, with $dp/dx = -$ constant. Integrate twice:
$$u(r) = -\frac{1}{4\mu}\frac{dp}{dx}(R^2 - r^2)$$
The parabolic profile (Hagen 1839, Poiseuille 1840) has maximum centreline velocity $u_{max} = -R^2/(4\mu)\cdot dp/dx$.

Volume flow rate:
$$Q = \int_0^R 2\pi r u\, dr = -\frac{\pi R^4}{8\mu}\frac{dp}{dx} = \frac{\pi R^4 \Delta p}{8\mu L}$$

**Limitations**:
1. **Laminar only**: $\text{Re} < 2300$ (White 2016).
2. **Fully-developed**: requires entry length $L_e \approx 0.06\,\text{Re}\,D$; for $\text{Re} = 1000$, $L_e = 60D$.
3. **Newtonian fluid**: non-Newtonian (e.g., polymer melts, blood — Bingham plastic) require different constitutive relations; see Metzner & Reed (1955).
4. **No-slip wall**: holds for $\text{Kn} < 0.1$; microchannels need slip correction.

**Engineering example**: hydraulic fluid flow in a 4 mm line, $Q = 0.5$ L/min, oil $\mu = 0.04$ Pa·s: $V = 0.66$ m/s, $\text{Re} = 66$, laminar. $\Delta p = 8\mu L Q/(\pi R^4)$. For $L = 2$ m: $\Delta p = 8\times0.04\times2\times 8.33\times10^{-6}/(\pi\times (2\times10^{-3})^4) = 42.4$ kPa.

### Q8. What are the conditions for flow separation and how does it affect aerodynamic performance?

**Detailed answer**:
**Separation criterion** (Prandtl 1904; Schlichting 1979): flow separates from a wall when the near-wall fluid lacks the kinetic energy to overcome an adverse pressure gradient ($dp/dx > 0$ in streamwise direction).

**Quantitative criterion**: at separation, the wall shear stress $\tau_w = \mu(\partial u/\partial y)|_{wall} = 0$. Beyond separation, $\tau_w < 0$ (reversed flow).

**Conditions promoting separation**:
1. Strong adverse pressure gradient (e.g., aft of cylinder at Re $\sim 10^4$).
2. Thick boundary layer (high-momentum deficit near wall).
3. Low Reynolds number (viscous sublayer thicker, less inertia).
4. Surface roughness (triggers early transition → thinner laminar BL but earlier turbulent separation).
5. Curvature adverse to flow (centrifugal force adds to pressure gradient).

**Aerodynamic consequences**:
- **Drag increase**: bluff-body separation creates low-pressure wake → form drag dominates.
- **Lift loss**: wing stall (G. I. Taylor 1927) at angle of attack $\alpha \approx 15°$–$20°$; flow separates on upper surface, suction peak collapses.
- **Buffet and vibration**: vortex shedding at Strouhal $\text{St} = fD/V \approx 0.2$ for cylinders (Roshko 1954) — Tacoma Narrows collapse 1940 was vortex-induced resonance.

**Control methods** (Gad-el-Hak 2000): vortex generators, suction, blowing, compliant surfaces, plasma actuators. For example, a golf ball's dimples (Bearman & Harvey 1976) trip the boundary layer turbulent, making it thinner at separation and reducing drag by ~50% compared to a smooth ball.

### Q9. Discuss the differences between RANS, LES, and DNS, and their computational cost.

**Detailed answer**:
The hierarchy of turbulence simulation methods:

| Method | What is solved | Turbulence model | Cost scaling | Typical cost (Re = 10⁴) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **DNS** | Full N-S, all scales | None | $\text{Re}^3$ | $10^{12}$ FLOPS |
| **LES** | Filtered N-S, subgrid | SGS model (Smagorinsky 1963) | $\text{Re}^{0.5}$–$\text{Re}^{1.2}$ | $10^6$–$10^8$ |
| **RANS** | Mean flow only | Closure model ($k$-$\epsilon$, SST) | $\text{Re}^{0}$–$\text{Re}^{0.3}$ | $10^3$–$10^4$ |

**DNS (Direct Numerical Simulation)** resolves all scales from integral length $L$ down to Kolmogorov scale $\eta = L\,\text{Re}^{-3/4}$ (Kolmogorov 1941a). No model. The grid requirement is $\sim \text{Re}^{9/4}$ (Pope 2000). Reserved for fundamental research; largest DNS to date: $\text{Re}_\tau = 10^5$ channel flow (Lee & Moser 2015, $1.4\times10^{12}$ grid points).

**LES (Large Eddy Simulation)** filters out scales smaller than grid spacing; subgrid-scale (SGS) model accounts for their effect. Standard SGS: Smagorinsky (1963) $L_s = (C_s \Delta)^2 |S|$. Wall-modelled LES (WMLES, Kawai & Larsson 2012) avoids resolving viscous sublayer, extending LES to high-Re industrial flows.

**RANS (Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes)** solves only the mean field with closure models. Most industrial CFD (90%+) uses RANS with $k$-$\epsilon$ (Launder & Spalding 1974) or SST $k$-$\omega$ (Menter 1994). Calibrated against benchmark experiments (ERCOFTAC, NASA turbulence database).

**Cost example** — aerodynamic flow over a wing at $\text{Re} = 10^6$:
- RANS: 5 million cells, 30 minutes on 64 cores.
- LES: 200 million cells, 1 week on 4096 cores.
- DNS: impossible — exceeds exascale by factor $10^{12}$.

### Q10. Explain the energy equation for viscous flow and why mechanical energy is dissipated.

**Detailed answer**:
Starting from the first law of thermodynamics for a fluid particle (Clausius 1865):
$$\frac{D}{Dt}(h + \frac{v^2}{2} + gz) = \frac{1}{\rho}\frac{Dp}{Dt} + \dot{q} - \dot{w}_s$$
where $h$ is enthalpy, $\dot{q}$ is heat addition, $\dot{w}_s$ is shaft work per unit mass.

Subtracting the mechanical energy equation (multiplied by density and integrated):
$$\frac{D}{Dt}\left(\frac{v^2}{2} + gz\right) = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla\cdot(p\vec{v}) + \vec{v}\cdot\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau} + \vec{v}\cdot\vec{g}$$

The viscous dissipation function:
$$\Phi = \tau_{ij}\frac{\partial v_i}{\partial x_j} = \mu\sum_{i,j}\left(\frac{\partial v_i}{\partial x_j}\right)^2 \quad (\text{for incompressible Newtonian})$$

Energy balance per unit volume:
$$\rho\frac{Dh}{Dt} = \frac{DP}{Dt} + \nabla\cdot(k\nabla T) + \Phi$$

**Why mechanical energy dissipates**: $\Phi \geq 0$ always for Newtonian fluids (by definition of viscosity). This is the **second law of thermodynamics** in mechanical disguise — viscous stresses always convert ordered kinetic energy into internal (thermal) energy, never the reverse. For incompressible laminar pipe flow, integrating $\Phi$ over the volume gives $\Phi_{total} = \Delta p \cdot Q$, exactly equating pressure work to frictional heating.

**Engineering implication**: A pump delivering 100 L/min at 100 kPa pressure drop dissipates $\Phi = 100\times10^{-3}/60 \times 100\times10^3 = 167$ W as heat. This is why hydraulic systems need oil coolers; adiabatic temperature rise would be $\Delta T = 167/(c_p \dot{m}) = 167/(2000 \times 870 \times 100\times10^{-3}/60) = 5.8$ K/min — would overheat in ~10 minutes.

## DD-1. Turbulent Boundary Layer: The Modern View (紊流邊界層的現代觀點)

### EN
The turbulent boundary layer is the most important practical flow in engineering — it covers aircraft wings, turbine blades, ship hulls, and pipe walls at industrial Reynolds numbers. Modern DNS databases (Lee & Moser 2015 at $\text{Re}_\tau = 1000$–$10000$; channel flow; Jiménez 2013 for pipe flow) have revolutionised understanding.

**Inner scaling** (wall units, $y^+ = y u_\tau/\nu$, $u^+ = u/u_\tau$):
- Viscous sublayer: $y^+ < 5$, $u^+ = y^+$
- Buffer layer: $5 < y^+ < 30$, complex dynamics
- Logarithmic layer: $30 < y^+ < 0.1\,\text{Re}_\tau$, $u^+ = (1/\kappa)\ln y^+ + B$ with $\kappa = 0.41$ (von Kármán constant, Nikuradse 1932; revised by Nagib & Chauhan 2008 to $\kappa = 0.392$), $B \approx 5.0$.
- Outer layer: $y/\delta \sim 1$, profile $u^+ = (1/\kappa)\ln y^+ + B + $ wake correction.

**Coherent structures**: Hairpin vortices (Theodorsen 1952; Adrian 2007) are the dominant eddies, organised in packets spanning $\sim 200$ wall units. They contribute ~50% of Reynolds stress and dominate near-wall mixing.

### 中文
紊流邊界層係工程上最重要嘅實用流場——覆蓋機翼、渦輪葉片、船體同管道壁面嘅高雷諾數流動。現代直接數值模擬（Lee & Moser 2015, $\text{Re}_\tau = 1000$–$10000$）徹底改變咗我哋嘅理解。

**內尺度**（壁面單位，$y^+ = yu_\tau/\nu$，$u^+ = u/u_\tau$）：
- 黏性子層：$y^+ < 5$，$u^+ = y^+$
- 緩衝層：$5 < y^+ < 30$，動力學複雜
- 對數層：$30 < y^+ < 0.1\text{Re}_\tau$，$u^+ = (1/\kappa)\ln y^+ + B$，其中 $\kappa = 0.41$（von Kármán 常數，Nikuradse 1932），$B \approx 5.0$
- 外層：$y/\delta \sim 1$，含尾跡修正

**相干結構**：髮夾渦（Theodorsen 1952; Adrian 2007）係主導渦結構，跨越約 200 個壁面單位，貢獻約 50% 嘅雷諾應力，係近壁混合嘅主體。

## DD-2. Open-Channel Flow and Hydraulic Jump (明渠流與水躍)

### EN
Open-channel flow (Chow 1959) occurs when a free surface is present and pressure is atmospheric. The dimensionless Froude number governs:
$$\text{Fr} = \frac{V}{\sqrt{gh}}$$
- $\text{Fr} < 1$: subcritical, surface waves travel upstream.
- $\text{Fr} = 1$: critical, minimum specific energy.
- $\text{Fr} > 1$: supercritical, surface disturbances swept downstream.

The **hydraulic jump** (Bidone 1819; Bélanger 1828) is a stationary dissipative transition from super- to subcritical:
$$\frac{h_2}{h_1} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{1 + 8\text{Fr}_1^2} - 1\right)$$
Energy dissipated: $\Delta E = \frac{(h_2 - h_1)^3}{4 h_1 h_2}$. For $\text{Fr}_1 = 4$: $h_2/h_1 = 2.46$, dissipation = 32% of upstream energy — used in stilling basins to dissipate flood energy.

### 中文
明渠流（Chow 1959）出現於有自由液面且壓力為大氣壓嘅流動。弗汝德數決定流態：
$$\text{Fr} = \frac{V}{\sqrt{gh}}$$
- $\text{Fr} < 1$：亞臨界流，表面波可向上游傳播
- $\text{Fr} = 1$：臨界流，比能最小
- $\text{Fr} > 1$：超臨界流，表面擾動向下游傳播

**水躍**（Bidone 1819; Bélanger 1828）係超臨界流向亞臨界流嘅靜止耗散躍遷：
$$\frac{h_2}{h_1} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{1 + 8\text{Fr}_1^2} - 1\right)$$
能量耗散：$\Delta E = \frac{(h_2 - h_1)^3}{4 h_1 h_2}$。當 $\text{Fr}_1 = 4$：$h_2/h_1 = 2.46$，耗散佔上游能量 32%——常用於消能池消散洪水能量。

## DD-3. Compressible Flow and Shock Waves (可壓流與衝擊波)

### EN
When Mach number $\text{Ma} = V/c_s \gtrsim 0.3$, compressibility matters (Liepmann & Roshko 1957). The area-velocity relation for isentropic flow:
$$\frac{A}{A^*} = \frac{1}{\text{Ma}}\left[\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}\text{Ma}^2\right)\right]^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)}}$$
has a minimum at $\text{Ma} = 1$ (choked flow, $A = A^*$).

Across a normal shock (Rankine 1870; Hugoniot 1887):
$$\text{Ma}_2^2 = \frac{1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}\text{Ma}_1^2}{\gamma\text{Ma}_1^2 - \frac{\gamma-1}{2}}, \quad \frac{p_2}{p_1} = 1 + \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}(\text{Ma}_1^2 - 1)$$
Total pressure loss: $p_{0,2}/p_{0,1} = f(\text{Ma}_1, \gamma)$, ~10% at $\text{Ma}_1 = 1.5$.

**Oblique shocks** (θ-β-M relation, Meyer 1908):
$$\tan\theta = 2\cot\beta\frac{\text{Ma}_1^2\sin^2\beta - 1}{\text{Ma}_1^2(\gamma + \cos 2\beta) + 2}$$
Wedge flow at $\text{Ma}_1 = 2$, $\theta = 15°$: $\beta = 33.2°$, $\text{Ma}_2 = 1.65$.

### 中文
當馬赫數 $\text{Ma} = V/c_s \gtrsim 0.3$ 時，可壓縮性不可忽略（Liepmann & Roshko 1957）。等熵流嘅面積-速度關係式：
$$\frac{A}{A^*} = \frac{1}{\text{Ma}}\left[\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}\text{Ma}^2\right)\right]^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)}}$$
喺 $\text{Ma} = 1$（壅塞流，$A = A^*$）處有最小值。

跨越正激波（Rankine 1870; Hugoniot 1887）：
$$\text{Ma}_2^2 = \frac{1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}\text{Ma}_1^2}{\gamma\text{Ma}_1^2 - \frac{\gamma-1}{2}}, \quad \frac{p_2}{p_1} = 1 + \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}(\text{Ma}_1^2 - 1)$$
總壓損失：$p_{0,2}/p_{0,1} = f(\text{Ma}_1, \gamma)$，$\text{Ma}_1 = 1.5$ 時約 10%。

**斜激波**（θ-β-M 關係，Meyer 1908）：
$$\tan\theta = 2\cot\beta\frac{\text{Ma}_1^2\sin^2\beta - 1}{\text{Ma}_1^2(\gamma + \cos 2\beta) + 2}$$
$\text{Ma}_1 = 2$、$\theta = 15°$ 嘅楔形流：$\beta = 33.2°$，$\text{Ma}_2 = 1.65$。

## DD-4. Dimensional Analysis and Similitude (因次分析與相似原理)

### EN
Dimensional analysis (Maxwell 1873; Buckingham 1914; Langhaar 1951) provides the systematic tool for scaling experiments.

**Procedure** (Rayleigh 1877; Buckingham 1914):
1. List all variables $q_1, q_2, \ldots, q_n$ with their physical dimensions $[q_i]$.
2. Choose $k$ repeating variables spanning all $r$ fundamental dimensions.
3. Form $n - k$ dimensionless groups $\Pi_i = q_i \prod (q_j)^{a_{ij}}$.
4. Express the result as $\Pi_m = f(\Pi_1, \ldots, \Pi_{m-1})$.

**Key dimensionless groups in fluid mechanics**:
$$\text{Re} = \frac{\rho V L}{\mu}, \quad \text{Fr} = \frac{V}{\sqrt{gL}}, \quad \text{Ma} = \frac{V}{c_s}, \quad \text{Eu} = \frac{p}{\rho V^2}$$
$$\text{Pr} = \frac{\nu}{\alpha}, \quad \text{We} = \frac{\rho V^2 L}{\sigma}, \quad \text{St} = \frac{fL}{V}, \quad \text{Gr} = \frac{g\beta \Delta T L^3}{\nu^2}$$

For complete similitude, all groups must match. In practice, only the dominant 1–2 are matched, leading to partial similarity (e.g., Reynolds-number-matched aerodynamics without Froude for wind tunnels).

### 中文
因次分析（Maxwell 1873; Buckingham 1914; Langhaar 1951）係實驗縮放嘅系統工具。

**步驟**（Rayleigh 1877; Buckingham 1914）：
1. 列出所有變量 $q_1, q_2, \ldots, q_n$ 及其物理因次 $[q_i]$
2. 選擇 $k$ 個循環變量，覆蓋所有 $r$ 個基本因次
3. 構造 $n - k$ 個無因次群 $\Pi_i = q_i \prod (q_j)^{a_{ij}}$
4. 結果表達為 $\Pi_m = f(\Pi_1, \ldots, \Pi_{m-1})$

**流體力學嘅關鍵無因次群**：
$$\text{Re} = \frac{\rho V L}{\mu}, \quad \text{Fr} = \frac{V}{\sqrt{gL}}, \quad \text{Ma} = \frac{V}{c_s}, \quad \text{Eu} = \frac{p}{\rho V^2}$$
$$\text{Pr} = \frac{\nu}{\alpha}, \quad \text{We} = \frac{\rho V^2 L}{\sigma}, \quad \text{St} = \frac{fL}{V}, \quad \text{Gr} = \frac{g\beta \Delta T L^3}{\nu^2}$$

完全相似要求所有無因次群匹配。實踐中通常只匹配主導嘅 1–2 個，造成部分相似（例如風洞嘅雷諾數匹配，但忽略弗汝德數）。

## DD-5. Non-Newtonian Fluid Mechanics (非牛頓流體力學)

### EN
Non-Newtonian fluids (Wilkinson 1960; Bird, Armstrong & Hassager 1987) deviate from Newton's linear $\tau = \mu\dot{\gamma}$ law. Constitutive classification:

**Time-independent**:
- Pseudoplastic (shear-thinning): $\tau = K\dot{\gamma}^n$ with $n < 1$; e.g., ketchup, blood, polymer solutions. Ostwald-de Waele (1923) power-law model.
- Dilatant (shear-thickening): $n > 1$; e.g., cornstarch suspensions.
- Bingham plastic: yield stress $\tau_y$ then linear; e.g., toothpaste, mud. Herschel-Bulkley model:
$$\tau = \tau_y + K\dot{\gamma}^n$$

**Time-dependent**:
- Thixotropic: viscosity decreases over time under shear (yogurt).
- Rheopectic: viscosity increases under shear.

**Viscoelastic**: combine viscous and elastic response; Maxwell fluid:
$$\tau + \lambda\dot{\tau} = \mu\dot{\gamma}$$
Weissenberg number $\text{Wi} = \lambda\dot{\gamma}$ measures elasticity. Polymer melts, biological fluids (Oldroyd 1950).

**Pipe flow of power-law fluid** (Metzner & Reed 1955): modified Reynolds number
$$\text{Re}_{gen} = \frac{D^n V^{2-n} \rho}{8^{n-1} K\left(\frac{3n+1}{4n}\right)^n}$$
allows use of friction-factor charts with laminar $f = 64/\text{Re}_{gen}$.

### 中文
非牛頓流體（Wilkinson 1960; Bird, Armstrong & Hassager 1987）偏離牛頓嘅線性定律 $\tau = \mu\dot{\gamma}$。本構分類：

**時間無關型**：
- 假塑性（剪切稀化）：$\tau = K\dot{\gamma}^n$，$n < 1$；如茄汁、血液、聚合物溶液。Ostwald-de Waele（1923）冪律模型。
- 脹流型（剪切增稠）：$n > 1$；如粟米澱粉懸浮液。
- Bingham 塑性：屈服應力 $\tau_y$ 後線性；如牙膏、泥漿。Herschel-Bulkley 模型：
$$\tau = \tau_y + K\dot{\gamma}^n$$

**時間相關型**：
- 觸變性：剪切下黏度隨時間下降（乳酪）。
- 反觸變性：剪切下黏度隨時間上升。

**黏彈性**：黏性同彈性響應結合；Maxwell 流體：
$$\tau + \lambda\dot{\tau} = \mu\dot{\gamma}$$
Weissenberg 數 $\text{Wi} = \lambda\dot{\gamma}$ 衡量彈性。聚合物熔體、生物流體（Oldroyd 1950）。

**冪律流體嘅管道流**（Metzner & Reed 1955）：修正雷諾數
$$\text{Re}_{gen} = \frac{D^n V^{2-n} \rho}{8^{n-1} K\left(\frac{3n+1}{4n}\right)^n}$$
可用摩阻係數圖，層流 $f = 64/\text{Re}_{gen}$。

# 10SL — Ten Self-Test Solutions

### Q1. Hydraulic cylinder with $D = 50$ mm, oil $\mu = 0.04$ Pa·s, $V = 2$ m/s. Calculate Re and flow regime. If $Q = 300$ L/min, $d = 10$ mm, $L = 5$ m, find pressure loss.

**Solution**:
For cylinder flow: $\text{Re} = \rho V D/\mu$. With $\rho_{oil} = 870$ kg/m³:
$$\text{Re} = \frac{870 \times 2 \times 0.05}{0.04} = 2175$$
Flow regime: $\text{Re} < 2300$ → **laminar**, but very close to transition.

For pipe: $A = \pi d^2/4 = \pi (0.01)^2/4 = 7.85 \times 10^{-5}$ m². $V = Q/A = (300\times 10^{-3}/60)/7.85\times 10^{-5} = 63.7$ m/s. This is unreasonably high — the given $d = 10$ mm is unrealistically small for 300 L/min. A more realistic pipe: $d = 25$ mm gives $V = (5\times 10^{-3})/(4.91\times 10^{-4}) = 10.2$ m/s, $\text{Re} = 870 \times 10.2 \times 0.025/0.04 = 5540$ → turbulent.

Using Colebrook for steel pipe ($\varepsilon/d = 0.045/25 = 0.0018$): $f \approx 0.038$. Pressure loss:
$$\Delta p = f\frac{L}{D}\frac{\rho V^2}{2} = 0.038 \times \frac{5}{0.025} \times \frac{870 \times 10.2^2}{2} = 344 \text{ kPa}$$

### Q2. Derive Bernoulli's equation from Euler's integrated form; explain why Bernoulli is along-streamline only.

**Solution**:
Starting from Euler equation:
$$\frac{\partial \vec{v}}{\partial t} + (\vec{v}\cdot\nabla)\vec{v} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \vec{g}$$
For steady flow ($\partial \vec{v}/\partial t = 0$):
$$(\vec{v}\cdot\nabla)\vec{v} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \vec{g}$$
Using vector identity: $(\vec{v}\cdot\nabla)\vec{v} = \nabla(v^2/2) - \vec{v}\times\vec{\omega}$, where $\vec{\omega} = \nabla\times\vec{v}$ is vorticity.
$$\nabla(v^2/2) - \vec{v}\times\vec{\omega} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \vec{g}$$
Take dot product with $\vec{v}\cdot d\vec{r}$ along a streamline (where $d\vec{r} = \vec{v}\,dt$ and $\vec{v}\times\vec{\omega}\cdot\vec{v} = 0$ since cross product is perpendicular to $\vec{v}$):
$$\vec{v}\cdot\nabla(v^2/2) = -\frac{1}{\rho}\vec{v}\cdot\nabla p + \vec{v}\cdot\vec{g}$$
$$\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{v^2}{2} + \frac{p}{\rho} + gz\right) = 0 \implies \frac{p}{\rho} + \frac{v^2}{2} + gz = C$$
The constant $C$ is constant **along a single streamline**. In irrotational flow ($\vec{\omega}=0$), the term $\vec{v}\times\vec{\omega}$ vanishes everywhere and the equation holds across the entire flow field — this is why Bernoulli is often called the "irrotational flow equation" when used globally.

### Q3. Estimate boundary-layer separation on a 1 m flat plate with free-stream $V = 10$ m/s in air ($\nu = 1.5\times 10^{-5}$ m²/s), and compute total friction drag.

**Solution**:
End-of-plate Reynolds number: $\text{Re}_L = VL/\nu = 10 \times 1 / (1.5\times 10^{-5}) = 6.67\times 10^5$.
Critical Reynolds number: $\text{Re}_{x,c} \approx 5\times 10^5$, so transition occurs at $x_c = \text{Re}_{x,c}\nu/V = 5\times 10^5 \times 1.5\times 10^{-5}/10 = 0.75$ m.

**Laminar region** ($0 < x < 0.75$ m): average $C_f = 1.328/\sqrt{\text{Re}_L} = 1.328/\sqrt{6.67\times 10^5} = 1.625\times 10^{-3}$.
$$D_{lam} = C_f \cdot \frac{1}{2}\rho V^2 \cdot A = 1.625\times 10^{-3} \times 0.5 \times 1.2 \times 100 \times 0.75 \times W$$
where $W$ is the span. Per unit span: $D_{lam} = 1.625\times 10^{-3} \times 0.5 \times 1.2 \times 100 \times 0.75 = 0.073$ N/m.

**Turbulent region** ($0.75 < x < 1$ m): average turbulent $C_f$ from $\text{Re}_{x,c}$ to $\text{Re}_L$:
$$\bar{C}_f = 0.074/\text{Re}_L^{1/5} - 0.074/\text{Re}_{x,c}^{1/5}\cdot(x_c/L) = 0.074/(6.67\times 10^5)^{1/5} = 0.00455$$
More precisely, integrating: $\bar{C}_{f,turb} \approx 0.0044$ over the turbulent section.
$$D_{turb} = 0.0044 \times 0.5 \times 1.2 \times 100 \times 0.25 = 0.066 \text{ N/m}$$

Total friction drag per unit span: $D_{total} = 0.073 + 0.066 = 0.139$ N/m. (Note: on a flat plate with zero pressure gradient, separation does not occur; the question implicitly assumes smooth pressure gradient. If adverse gradient exists, separation requires von Kármán integral.)

### Q4. Explain pump-pipe system curves and how valve opening affects operating point.

**Solution**:
A centrifugal pump has a **pump curve** $H_p(Q)$ that decreases monotonically: $H_p = H_0 - kQ^2$ approximately, where $H_0$ is shut-off head. The **system curve** represents pipe-network head requirement:
$$H_s(Q) = \Delta z + \frac{V_{out}^2}{2g} + h_f(Q) = \Delta z + \frac{8Q^2}{\pi^2 g D_{out}^4} + K_{system}\frac{V^2}{2g}$$
where $K_{system}$ includes pipe friction, fittings, and valve. The operating point is the intersection $H_p(Q^*) = H_s(Q^*)$. A partially closed valve adds $K_{valve}\frac{V^2}{2g}$ to the system curve, raising $H_s$ at every $Q$; the intersection shifts to lower $Q^*$ and higher $H^*$. Specifically, $K_{valve}$ for a ball valve: 0.05 (fully open) to $>200$ (nearly closed). For a globe valve at 50% open, $K \approx 5.7$ (White 2016, Table 6.5). The pump still runs at the new operating point but **dissipates additional power $\Delta P = \rho g Q^* \Delta H$**, converted to heat at the valve.

### Q5. Derive $f = 64/\text{Re}$ for laminar pipe flow from N-S.

**Solution**:
Steady, fully-developed laminar flow in a circular pipe of radius $R$ has $u = u(r)$, $v = w = 0$. The N-S equations reduce to:
$$0 = -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{dp}{dx} + \nu\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r\frac{du}{dr}\right)$$
$dp/dx = -\Delta p/L = -\text{const}$. Integrate twice:
$$r\frac{du}{dr} = \frac{\Delta p}{2\mu L}r^2 + C_1$$
$$u(r) = \frac{\Delta p}{4\mu L}r^2 + C_1 \ln r + C_2$$
Boundary conditions: $u(R) = 0$ (no-slip), $u$ finite at $r=0$ → $C_1 = 0$. So $u(r) = -\frac{\Delta p}{4\mu L}(R^2 - r^2)$. Average velocity:
$$\bar{u} = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{1}{\pi R^2}\int_0^R 2\pi r \cdot \frac{\Delta p}{4\mu L}(R^2 - r^2)\, dr = \frac{\Delta p R^2}{8\mu L}$$
Friction factor: $h_f = \Delta p/(\rho g) = f(L/D)V^2/(2g)$, so:
$$f = \frac{2\Delta p D}{\rho L V^2} = \frac{2 \cdot 8\mu L V/(R^2) \cdot 2R}{\rho L V^2} = \frac{64\mu}{\rho V D} = \frac{64}{\text{Re}}$$

### Q6. Compute $C_D$ of a high-speed train head at $V = 100$ m/s, frontal area $A = 10$ m², $\rho = 1.2$ kg/m³, and power to overcome air drag.

**Solution**:
For a modern high-speed train head (e.g., Shinkansen N700S aerodynamic profile), $C_D \approx 0.20$ at $\text{Re} \sim 10^8$ based on train length (Suzuki & Nakade 2013). Drag force:
$$F_D = \frac{1}{2}\rho V^2 C_D A = 0.5 \times 1.2 \times 100^2 \times 0.20 \times 10 = 12,000 \text{ N}$$
Power: $P_D = F_D \times V = 12,000 \times 100 = 1.2$ MW per train — significant for electric traction design. **Note**: At $\text{Ma} = 100/343 = 0.29$, compressibility effects begin ($\rho$ increases ~4% from stagnation), but for engineering estimate incompressible formula is acceptable.

### Q7. Use Reynolds similarity to predict prototype behaviour from model testing; explain Froude and Mach regimes.

**Solution**:
Reynolds similarity requires $\text{Re}_m = \text{Re}_p$:
$$V_m L_m/\nu_m = V_p L_p/\nu_p$$
For a 1:5 scale aircraft model in the same air: $V_m = 5V_p$ to maintain Re. **Froude number** governs gravity-dominated free-surface flows (ships, hydraulics): $\text{Fr} = V/\sqrt{gL}$. Ships are tested in towing tanks at model Fr equal to prototype Fr; the resulting wave drag scales by displacement ratio. Froude 1870 originally developed this for HMS Greyhound trials.

**Mach number** governs compressibility: $\text{Ma} = V/c_s$. Transonic aerodynamics requires either (a) a pressurized wind tunnel (increases $c_s$), or (b) a cryogenic tunnel (decreases $T$, decreases $c_s$). Example: ONERA S1MA at Modane has $T = 100$ K, $c_s = 320$ m/s, so testing at $V = 100$ m/s gives $\text{Ma} = 0.31$, but the test Reynolds number at scale 1:50 of a transport is comparable to full-scale at cruise.

### Q8. Derive the 2D laminar flat-plate boundary-layer equations from N-S.

**Solution**:
For 2D steady incompressible flow with $v \ll u$:
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = 0$$
$$u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{dp}{dx} + \nu\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}$$
Boundary layer approximations (Prandtl 1904):
1. $v \ll u$, $\partial/\partial y \gg \partial/\partial x$ → drop $\partial^2 u/\partial x^2$ in viscous term.
2. External flow is inviscid, so $dp/dx = -V dV/dx = 0$ for flat plate.
3. Pressure gradient decouples from BL.
Final:
$$u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = \nu\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}$$
with $u = v = 0$ at $y = 0$ (no-slip, no-penetration), $u \to V$ as $y \to \infty$.
Similarity solution $\psi = \sqrt{\nu V x} \cdot f(\eta)$ with $\eta = y\sqrt{V/(\nu x)}$ yields Blasius's $f''(\eta) + 0.5 f(\eta) f''(\eta) = 0$ with $f(0) = f'(0) = 0$, $f'(\infty) = 1$. Numerical solution: $f''(0) = 0.332$, giving $\delta_{99} = 5.0\sqrt{\nu x/V}$, $\tau_w = 0.332\mu V/\sqrt{\nu x/V} = 0.332 \rho V^2/\sqrt{\text{Re}_x}$, hence $C_{f,x} = 0.664/\sqrt{\text{Re}_x}$.

### Q9. Discuss microchannel flow (Knudsen regime transitions) compared to macroscopic flow.

**Solution**:
Microchannel flow (Kandlikar, Garimella, Li, Colin & King 2013) exhibits distinct phenomena when $D < 100\ \mu$m:
1. **Continuum breakdown**: $\text{Kn} = \lambda_{mfp}/D > 0.001$. At $D = 10\ \mu$m in air, $\lambda_{mfp} = 68$ nm → $\text{Kn} = 0.007$ (slip flow).
2. **Slip at wall**: Navier-Stokes-Maxwell (Maxwell 1879): $u_{slip} = L_s \tau_w/\mu$, where $L_s = (2-\sigma_v)/\sigma_v \cdot \lambda_{mfp} \cdot C$ (Knudsen 1909; Tang 2007). $\sigma_v$ is tangential momentum accommodation coefficient (often $\approx 1$).
3. **Surface-to-volume ratio increases**: friction factor rises with rarefaction; for $\text{Kn} = 0.05$ (typical MEMS channel), $f \approx 96/\text{Re}$ vs. $64/\text{Re}$ for macro (Arkilic & Breuer 1993).
4. **Entrance effects dominate**: $L_e \sim D\,\text{Re}$; for $D = 50\ \mu$m, $L_e \approx 5$ mm — same as entire channel length.
5. **Surface-charge/electrokinetic effects**: EDL (electric double layer) thickness $\lambda_D \sim 1$–$10$ nm for typical electrolytes; zeta potential drives electroosmotic flow (Smoluchowski 1903).
6. **Compressibility in gas microchannels**: rarefaction effects dominate; the Fanno and Rayleigh flow regimes differ (Beskok & Karniadakis 1999).
